NASA RAG Chat — Evaluation Dataset
=====================================
Three sample questions used to evaluate the RAG system against prior knowledge.
Each entry contains: the question, the expected answer based on historical knowledge,
the actual answer returned by the RAG system, and a remark on the difference.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
QUESTION 1
------------------------------------------------------------------------
What were the names of the Apollo 11 command module and lunar module?

EXPECTED ANSWER:
The Apollo 11 command module was named "Columbia" and the lunar module was
named "Eagle." These call signs were chosen by the crew and became iconic —
"Eagle" gave rise to Neil Armstrong's famous words "The Eagle has landed"
upon touchdown at the Sea of Tranquility on July 20, 1969.

ACTUAL RAG ANSWER:
The Apollo 11 command module was named CSM-107, and the lunar module was named
LM-5. These details are provided in the documents referred to as "Apollo
Spacecraft Flight History" and "Appendix B - Vehicle Descriptions."

REMARK:
The RAG system returned the hardware serial designations (CSM-107, LM-5) from
technical archive documents, rather than the mission call signs (Columbia,
Eagle). Both answers are technically accurate in different contexts: the call
signs are how the vehicles were known during the mission and in public
communication, while the hardware numbers appear in engineering documentation.
The system correctly cited its sources. A retrieval improvement would be to
also surface the mission transcript chunks where "Columbia" and "Eagle" are
used directly by the crew.

QUESTION 2
------------------------------------------------------------------------
What caused the oxygen tank explosion on Apollo 13?

EXPECTED ANSWER:
The root cause was damaged Teflon insulation on wiring inside oxygen tank 2.
During pre-launch ground testing at the Kennedy Space Center, the tank was
inadvertently subjected to higher voltage (65V) than it was rated for (28V)
when ground crews tried to boil off residual oxygen. This burned away the
Teflon insulation on internal wires. During the mission on April 13, 1970,
when the crew performed a routine tank stir, the bare wires sparked inside
the oxygen-rich environment, igniting the remaining Teflon and causing an
explosion that ruptured the tank and severely damaged the service module.

ACTUAL RAG ANSWER:
The explosion was caused by an ignition of the Teflon insulation inside the
oxygen tank due to high voltage applied to the heater. Specifically, an exposed
wire inside oxygen tank 2 ignited the Teflon insulation. This event led to a
subsequent chain of events resulting in the explosion. The system cited the
"Apollo 13 Mission Commentary" transcript.

REMARK:
The RAG system's answer is substantively correct and matches the expected answer
well. It correctly identified the high-voltage event and the Teflon insulation
as the ignition source. The phrasing "exposed wire ignited the Teflon
insulation" is slightly imprecise (the damaged Teflon on the wires was itself
the fuel), but the causal chain is accurate. The answer is appropriately
grounded in the mission documents and cites its source. This is a good
demonstration of the system retrieving factually dense technical content.

QUESTION 3
------------------------------------------------------------------------
At what time did the Challenger STS-51L launch, and what was the outside
temperature at launch?

EXPECTED ANSWER:
The Challenger STS-51L launched at 11:38 AM Eastern Standard Time on
January 28, 1986, from Launch Complex 39B at Kennedy Space Center. The
ambient air temperature at launch time was approximately 36°F (2°C), which
was the coldest launch in the shuttle program's history up to that point.
Overnight temperatures had dropped to around 24–27°F, causing ice to form
on the launch structure. The cold conditions were a critical factor: the
low temperature caused the O-rings in the right solid rocket booster to
become brittle and fail to seal properly, leading to the disaster
73 seconds after liftoff.

ACTUAL RAG ANSWER:
The launch was tentatively planned for 10:38 AM Eastern Time. The temperature
at the launch pad right before launch was 24 degrees Fahrenheit. The system
cited the STS-51L Mission Audio transcript, quoting a PAO announcement about
cloud cover, wind, and temperature conditions.

REMARK:
The RAG system's answer is partially correct but contains two notable
discrepancies. First, 10:38 AM was an earlier planned launch time (delayed
from the original January 27 attempt); the actual liftoff occurred at
11:38 AM EST. The transcript chunk retrieved likely referred to a pre-launch
status update rather than the moment of launch. Second, 24°F was the
overnight low — by the time of actual liftoff, ambient temperature had risen
to approximately 36°F, though the O-rings were still near freezing due to
the ice overnight. This example illustrates a limitation of RAG on
time-sensitive sequential data: the system retrieved a genuine excerpt from
the transcript but from an earlier point in the timeline than the question
intended. Increasing the number of retrieved chunks (n_results) or adding
metadata filtering by time segment could improve accuracy on questions like
this.
